Maths Behind EV Adoption Questioned

Tristan Wiggill May 29, 2026 EV Adoption

South Africa's electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure is improving and the appetite for EV's among fleet operators is genuine.

And yet, for the overwhelming majority of South African motorists and logistics companies, the business case for battery electric vehicles remains stubbornly difficult to close.

That was the clear-eyed assessment from a recent Isuzu Motors South Africa webinar, in which company president Billy Tom and industry policy expert Justin Barnes examined where new energy vehicles realistically stand in a market shaped by high interest rates, volatile fuel costs and an underdeveloped public charging network.

"Charging infrastructure is a necessary condition. I don't think it's a sufficient condition," Barnes noted. The challenge with battery technology, he argued, runs deeper than whether a driver can find a charge point between Johannesburg and Durban.

According to him, the economics simply do not pencil out for most buyers without government support. "Quite bluntly, I can find very few instances where it makes sense to have a battery electric motor car in South Africa. If there are subsidies, that might change the equation. But at the moment, you've got to care deeply about the environment and have deep pockets to justify battery electric over an ICE equivalent."

The United States experience reinforces his point. Much of Tesla's early momentum was built on a federal subsidy of around $7,500. Buyers who took that deal were, in many cases, making a rational financial decision rather than an environmental one.

Without comparable fiscal support in South Africa, the premium attached to an electric vehicle is difficult to absorb in a market where affordability pressures have been severe for several years running.

The commercial case is more promising, but complexity runs deep

If the passenger-vehicle numbers are discouraging, the commercial picture is more nuanced. 

Tom said that some of his bakery-sector customers have been pushing for compressed natural gas trucks, partly because they operate CNG in their production facilities and can refuel on site, and partly because of the environmental benefits over diesel.

Dual-fuel configurations, running CNG alongside diesel, are currently operating in certain fleets. He did, however, concede that battery-electric trucks have also generated genuine interest. "Two years ago we had a road show where we showed our first battery-electric trucks and tried to gauge appetite from the customers. They've been very keen," Tom said.

That enthusiasm, though, runs into hard physical and financial constraints. Battery weight is a significant issue for freight operators. On a vehicle travelling from Durban to Johannesburg, a heavy battery pack can mean losing a tonne and a half of payload capacity over a 600-kilometre run.

Barnes added that the weight problem reshapes the entire commercial use case. An electric bus running a fixed urban route with a light load can work exceptionally well. But the moment it carries a heavy or variable load over long distances, the battery's energy draw changes its application entirely.

Ultra-fast chargers, capable of recharging in 12 to 15 minutes, could compensate for some of this, but the technology and infrastructure investment required remains substantial.

Tom put the operator's dilemma plainly. "If I'm going to run an electric truck, do I have enough infrastructure from Durban to Joburg where I can fill up my truck? If I don't have, what's the point of doing it?"

There is also the residual value question, which fleet owners in South Africa take extremely seriously. Tom noted that commercial buyers want certainty about what a battery truck will be worth after three years of service, and that answer is still not available with confidence.

The picture that emerged from the discussion is of a transition that is inevitable in direction, uncertain in timing, and highly dependent on policy support that South Africa has yet to provide.
