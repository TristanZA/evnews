Why Chinese EVs Are Cheaper

Tristan Wiggill May 25, 2026 Industry Reports

Electric vehicles made in China consistently cost far less than comparable models produced elsewhere in the world. A new analysis from the IEA Global EV Outlook 2026 sheds light on the structural reasons behind this price gap, and the findings are more nuanced than many observers might expect.

The most obvious explanation that comes to mind is government subsidies. China has indeed offered generous financial support to its EV industry over many years, and preferential financing helped Chinese manufacturers scale up their operations faster than their international rivals.

Subsidies have played a meaningful role in shaping the industry, and analysts acknowledge they likely still matter today. However, the IEA's findings make clear that subsidies alone are far from a sufficient explanation for the pricing gap that exists today.

Labour costs are another contributing factor. Building an internal combustion engine vehicle in China costs roughly 30 per cent less than doing so in Germany, and labour rates in China run approximately three to five times lower than in many advanced economies. This is a genuine advantage, though it too, tells only part of the story.

China's Battery Advantage 

The single most important reason Chinese EVs are cheaper comes down to batteries. Chinese manufacturers have spent well over a decade building deep expertise in every layer of battery production, from raw components and battery cells through to finished battery packs.

The scale of this accumulated knowledge is reflected in the numbers. Manufacturing efficiency alone accounts for more than 40 per cent of the battery production cost gap between China and Europe.

Chinese producers have also embraced lithium iron phosphate, or LFP, battery chemistry far more widely than manufacturers elsewhere. LFP batteries avoid the expensive nickel-based materials that remain standard in many Western and South Korean battery designs. The result is a cheaper battery that is also well suited to the mass-market vehicles that dominate Chinese EV sales.

Factory scale reinforces every one of these advantages. Some Chinese EV plants are now capable of producing over one million vehicles per year. At that volume, fixed costs shrink dramatically on a per-vehicle basis, and the efficiency gains that come with repetition compound across the entire supply chain.

One factor that turns out to matter far less than popular perception suggests is energy costs. Electricity and fuel expenses account for only one to four per cent of total vehicle manufacturing costs, meaning that cheaper power in China is a negligible contributor to the price difference.

For South African consumers and fleet buyers watching the global EV market, the implications are significant. Chinese brands are already arriving on local shores at price points that put EVs within reach of a far wider audience than before.

The IEA analysis suggests this is not a temporary phenomenon driven by political support alone. The scale, the supply chain integration, the battery leadership and the manufacturing experience that underpin Chinese EV pricing have taken years to build, and closing the gap will require other regions to invest seriously in the same foundations over the long term.
