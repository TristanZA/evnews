SA's grid problem is also its EV opportunity

Tristan Wiggill May 14, 2026 Industry Reports

South Africa sits in an uncomfortable position in Africa's electric vehicle story. According to the Africa E-Mobility Alliance's latest continental signals report, the country's average annual power outage stands at 48.5 hours, the highest among the eleven leading African e-mobility markets tracked.

Rwanda, by comparison, averages just 8.5 hours. On the surface, that looks like a serious handicap for EV adoption. Look more carefully, though, and it tells a different story.

The report, drawn from Africa E-Mobility Week held in Addis Ababa in October 2025, argues that the continent's e-mobility transition has moved well past the question of whether electric vehicles can work in African conditions. The question now is how fast the transition can scale, and which markets are positioned to lead it.

The grid as a forcing function

South Africa's load shedding years have driven rooftop solar installations and battery storage adoption at a pace few other markets can match.

The result is a growing base of energy-independent households and businesses that are, in practical terms, already well suited to home EV charging. The infrastructure is being built from the bottom up, driven by necessity rather than policy.

This matters because the AfEMA report identifies integration as the defining challenge of the decade ahead. Charging infrastructure requires grid coordination. Battery deployment requires lifecycle management. Fleet electrification requires energy planning alongside vehicle procurement.

The report is clear that these are integration problems rather than technology problems, and South Africa's private sector has been solving integration problems under pressure for years.

South Africa also has more of the formal building blocks in place than most African markets. It already has a national e-mobility strategy, active fiscal incentives, and Extended Producer Responsibility frameworks covering lithium battery disposal.

It is one of only three African markets, alongside Kenya and Rwanda, to have adopted meaningful battery lifecycle regulation — a distinction the report flags as increasingly important as deployment scales.

A continental moment arriving in Cape Town

More than 150,000 EVs have been deployed across leading African markets, with over $620 million in disclosed investment flowing into the sector since 2020. Battery swap networks are scaling rapidly, with Spiro alone operating more than 1,200 stations across six countries.

That widening gap makes the next two years consequential for South Africa. The country will host Africa E-Mobility Week in Cape Town in September 2026, a signal in itself that it is regarded as a serious player in the continental conversation — a market with the institutional depth and private sector capacity to anchor the region's most significant e-mobility gathering.

The honest picture is that South Africa's grid constraints are a genuine obstacle requiring serious investment and coordination. The more interesting observation is that those same constraints have accelerated exactly the kind of distributed, integrated energy thinking that the EV transition ultimately depends on.

The country has been stress-tested in ways that others have not. That experience, if channelled well, is a foundation rather than a liability.
