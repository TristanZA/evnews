Electric Fleets Cross Competitive Cost Thresholds

Tristan Wiggill May 27, 2026 fleet electrification

South African commercial fleet operators that have made the switch to electric vehicles are recording measurable savings across fuel, maintenance and total cost of ownership (TCO), according to data presented by Ndia Magadagela, CEO of Everlectric, at a sector webinar hosted by EE Business Intelligence.

Drawing on a portfolio of real-world fleet deployments spanning more than 12.5 million kilometres of electric driving across clients including DSV, FedEx, Woolworths, Vodacom and Sanitech, Magadagela's presentation made a detailed financial case for commercial fleet electrification that goes well beyond environmental aspiration.

"Commercial fleets have done the maths," Magadagela told the audience of roughly 850 registered delegates. "They have seen that there are clearer economics. It is cheaper to run. And they have seen that it does not affect their downtime."

Those fleets have collectively saved an estimated 2.75 million kilograms of carbon emissions since deployment began, with Everlectric reporting 100 percent operational availability and zero mechanical downtime across its managed fleet.

Where the savings are found

The core of Magadagela's argument rested on a rigorous TCO analysis comparing the Maxus eDeliver3 electric panel van against the Isuzu D-Max across a modelled five-year lifecycle and 240,000 kilometres. 

On a direct purchase basis, the electric vehicle's TCO came in at R860,000 against R1.26 million for the equivalent ICE vehicle, a saving of approximately 27 percent. On a financed basis, the gap narrowed somewhat, with EV TCO at R1.04 million against R1.28 million for the ICE alternative, still representing a 23 percent advantage.

The figures account for the full cost base, including vehicle price, insurance, fuel versus electricity, service and maintenance, tyre costs, and residual value. Electric vehicles carry a higher upfront capital cost, with the Maxus eDeliver3 coming in at roughly 70 percent above its ICE equivalent before import duties are factored in. The savings, however, accumulate through dramatically lower operating costs.

Electricity costs in the analysis were modelled at R0.45 per kilometre for the EV against R2.41 per kilometre for the diesel vehicle, an efficiency gap of approximately 80 percent in favour of electric. 

Maintenance costs were calculated at R0.41 per kilometre for the EV against R0.76 per kilometre for the ICE vehicle, reflecting the significantly fewer moving parts in an electric powertrain. EVs also demonstrated a superior residual value in the model, with a projected return of R195,000 against R96,000 for the ICE equivalent.

"Additional savings are possible given that EV costing and efficiencies continue to improve," Magadagela noted, "with significant savings expected from 2025 technology."

The crossover point

A critical insight from the presentation was the identification of monthly kilometre thresholds at which operating an electric vehicle becomes cheaper than its diesel counterpart in a fully maintained lease structure. 

For the one-tonne segment, comparing the Maxus eDeliver3 against the Isuzu D-Max, Toyota Hilux and Volkswagen Caddy, the crossover point using 2025 cost data sits at approximately 3,200 kilometres per month. 

For the four-tonne segment, the Maxus eDeliver 75 outperforms all ICE competitors from around 2,500 kilometres per month. In the eight-tonne bracket, the same EV crossover dynamic applies at broadly similar thresholds.

A further comparison between the BYD Dolphin Surf and the Hyundai i10 Cargo, conducted using 2026 data, found a breakeven point of 2,685 kilometres per month, beyond which the electric vehicle is consistently the more affordable option to run.

The data also tested oil price sensitivity on the four-tonne segment. At a diesel price of R22 per litre, the eDeliver 75 becomes cheaper than the average diesel equivalent from around 2,500 kilometres per month. 

At R28 per litre, the crossover arrives earlier; at R35 per litre, the electric advantage widens substantially. Given the global trajectory of liquid fuel prices, Magadagela's sensitivity analysis suggested the EV case would only strengthen over time.

Removing barriers through a service model

Magadagela acknowledged that several structural challenges had historically slowed fleet electrification in South Africa, among them limited model availability, concerns about grid stability, prohibitive upfront capital costs, and the complexity of managing charging infrastructure. 

Everlectric's response has been to structure its offering as an electric vehicle as a service model, delivering the vehicle, dedicated on-site DC fast charging infrastructure, renewable electricity supply, full maintenance, and a cloud-based fleet management application under a single monthly invoice.

"This model allows an electric vehicle user or commercial fleet to use an electric vehicle, have charging infrastructure dedicated to them, have all their maintenance already worked into the solution, and have a driver-facing app that allows them to do their deliveries without the headache of knowing where to charge, when to charge, or when electricity is cheapest," she said.

The approach has proved particularly effective for hub-and-spoke operations, where vehicles return to a central depot for overnight or scheduled charging and predictable daily range requirements reduce range anxiety as a practical concern.

Magadagela said the availability of electric commercial vehicle models had improved considerably in recent years, spanning panel vans, trucks and passenger transport, reducing a barrier that would have prevented fleet adoption only a few years ago.

South Africa's advance in distributed solar generation had further reinforced the business case, with co-located solar and charging infrastructure now common across early adopter sites.
